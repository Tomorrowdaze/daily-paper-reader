## Abstract 7

Observed group sizes rarely match the size that would maximize what each member gets from 8

belonging. We propose a two -part theory in which group size is regulated by two related conflicts: 9

insider -outsider conflict over admission, and within -group conflict as crowding, competition, and 10 

social tensions intensify with size. Three strategies are available: admission, exclusion, and fission. 11 

The first part shows that even when exclusion is unavailable, fission dynamics alone drive group 12 

size away from the optimum in both directions, with the pattern set by how prospective joiners 13 

encounter groups and by the geometry of fission. When joiners compare groups across a shared 14 

landscape and fission is asymmetric , the standing distribution is bimodal: supra -optimal large 15 

groups coexisting with a sub -optimal mode of small groups , the pattern characteristic of fission -16 

fusion societies. The second part promotes exclusion and fission to active decisions: incumbents 17 

weigh the per -capita cost of accommodating entry ( β) against the costs of coordinated exclusion 18 

(c + γN *) and fissioning ( F). A single inequality, β > c + γN *, partitions populations into two 19 

regimes: where it holds, exclusion is viable and groups lock at the optimum size; where it fails, 20 

groups grow past the optimum and cycle through recurrent fission. Modal group size, fission 21 

frequency, and exclusion behavior together identify which regime governs a population — a set 22 

of predictions applicable across fishes, social insects, birds, and mammals including primates and 23 

human foragers. 24 

Keywords: group size; fission –fusion dynamics; insider –outsider conflict; optimal group size; 25 

collective action; coagulation –fragmentation 26 

## Introduction 27 

Understanding why animal groups deviate from their optimal size is one of the enduring problems 28 

in behavioral ecology. The benefits of group living, such as shared vigilance, cooperative foraging, 29 

and collective defense, are well established, as is the observation that per -capita returns from these 30 

benefits follow a hump -shaped function of group size: rising initially as groups grow, then 31 

declining as crowding and within -group competition erode the gains. The group size at which per -32 

capita returns are maximized is the nominal optimum, N* . Yet observed group sizes routinely 33 

deviate from N* , in both directions and often substantially, and the conditions under which they 34 

do so remain incompletely understood. 35 2

The supraoptimal case (groups larger than N* ) is the more theorized of the two. Sibly 36 

(1983) demonstrated that N* is individually unstable: groups grow beyond it through individually 37 

rational joining and settle at a larger Nash equilibrium size. Sibly’s result has become a cornerstone 38 

of group size theory and is broadly supported empirically. 39 

Sibly’s instability result was challenged shortly after publication by Giraldeau and Gillis 40 

(1985) , who showed that whether N* is stable depends on the shape of the per -capita fitness 41 

function: when the declining slope is steeper than the rising slope, groups do not grow past the 42 

optimum. We make this qualitative insight quantitatively precise below. Kramer (1985) extended 43 

the same debate to colonial breeders, a paradigmatic empirical test case for the supraoptimal -group 44 

hypothesis. 45 

The suboptimal case, groups smaller than N*, is empirically well documented and has been 46 

theorized mainly through exclusion. In wild baboon troops , for example , small groups bear greater 47 

costs relative to intermediate sizes (Markham et al. 2015) . Higashi and Yamamura (1993) 48 

formalized insider –outsider conflict as a strategic membership game in which equilibrium group 49 

size depends on the costs of joining versus exclusion: groups may settle below N* when the cost 50 

of defending the optimum exceeds the cost of preserving a smaller group. 51 

We argue that this framing overlooks a mechanism capable of generating suboptimal modal 52 

group size without any exclusion at all: the interaction between fission dynamics and joiner pool 53 

ecology. When a group splits, it produces a smaller resulting daughter below N*; the larger may 54 

remain above it, particularly when the split sheds only a small number of members. What happens 55 

next depends critically on whether prospective joiners arrive independently at each group from 56 

local catchments, or whether they choose among all available groups across a shared landscape. 57 

Under local joiner pools, daughters grow autonomously and modal group size settles above N* —58 

consistent with Sibly. Under a global joiner pool, daughters compete for the same joiner stream; if 59 

the split was symmetric this drives both daughters toward the fission threshold, leaving modal size 60 

supraoptimal rather than sub. Sub -optimal sizes instead emerge when fission is asymmetric: a 61 

crowded group sheds a few members while the bulk persists, and the resulting small daughter, far 62 

below N*, is the least attractive group in the pool and is bypassed by joiners while large groups 63 

continue to recruit. Suboptimal modal group size emerges from fission dynamics alone, with no 64 

exclusion required, when fission is asymmetric rather than symmetric. 65 

We develop the framework in two stages. The first treats fission as the sole regulatory 66 

mechanism and shows how the structure of the joiner pool (local versus global, preference -based 67 

versus random) determines whether the standing distribution of group sizes is unimodal near the 68 

optimum, bimodal with coexisting sub - and supra -optimal modes, or broadly over -dispersed. The 69 

same payoff function, fission threshold, and individual rationality assumptions produce opposite 70 

directional predictions depending on a single ecological parameter. The second extends the fission -71 

only model by introducing exclusion as a second regulatory mechanism, derives a viability 72 

condition that partitions a parameter space defined by exclusion costs, coordination burden, and 73 3

fission costs into two qualitatively distinct dynamic regimes, and shows how joiner pool ecology 74 

from the first part shapes which regime governs any given population. Together, the two models 75 

provide a unified framework for understanding the full range of observed group size deviations 76 

from the nominal optimum. 77 

## Background 78 

Optimal group size and the payoff function 79 

The theoretical foundation for group size research rests on a simple observation: per -capita benefits 80 

of group living are a non -monotonic function of group size. Pulliam and Caraco (1984) synthesized 81 

the early formal treatments, showing that per -capita returns rise from a solo baseline R1 as group 82 

size increases (e.g., because of shared vigilance, cooperative foraging, and collective defense) 83 

reach a maximum at the nominal optimum N* , and decline thereafter as crowding, within -group 84 

competition, and resource dilution intensify. The nominal optimum is thus the group size that 85 

maximizes individual fitness under steady ecological conditions. 86 

Throughout this manuscript, the per -capita payoff function Pᵢ( n) is treated as a component 87 

of inclusive fitness attributable to group membership at size n, following the phenotypic gambit 88 

(Grafen 1984) . Empirical tests of this framework span taxa from fish shoals (Pitcher 1986; Buston 89 

2003) to social insects (Grinsted and Field 2018a) to cooperatively breeding birds (Sturrock et al. 90 

2022) to rodents (Waterman 2002) to primates (Markham and Gesquiere 2017) to human foragers 91 

(Smith 1981) . The general shape of the payoff function is well supported, though estimates of N* 92 

vary substantially across populations and ecological contexts (Rodman 1981; Pulliam and Caraco 93 

1984) . What has proven more challenging to explain is not the location of N* but the systematic 94 

bidirectional divergence of realized modal group size from it. Recent reviews of the primate 95 

literature have flagged this divergence as an unresolved puzzle: long -term monitoring of wild 96 

populations shows groups persisting above their predicted optima, despite the theoretical 97 

expectation that prospective joiners should preferentially affiliate with optimally -sized groups 98 

(Markham and Gesquiere 2017) . Among human foragers, parallel puzzles have motivated active 99 

inquiry: the average forager group size far exceeds any non -human primate analog (David -Barrett 100 

2023) . Here we develop a theoretical account of why such persistence above and below the 101 

optimum is not anomalous but expected, under specified conditions on joiner pool ecology and the 102 

costs of coordinated exclusion. 103 

Supraoptimal group size: Sibly’s result and its extensions 104 

Clark and Mangel (1984) elaborated this logic in a more general setting, and the result has since 105 

been treated as the canonical prediction for social groups in which members cannot or do not 106 

exclude newcomers. It is broadly consistent with empirical findings that realized group sizes 107 

frequently exceed the group size at which per -capita fitness is maximized (Smith 1981; Markham 108 4

et al. 2015) . This logic is especially relevant to fission -fusion species: groups that repeatedly split 109 

and regrow will spend most of their cycle above N* , approaching the fission threshold before 110 

splitting again. 111 

Suboptimal group size: insider -outsider conflict and exclusion 112 

Subsequent theoretical work has refined the conditions under which exclusion succeeds (Shen et 113 

al. 2023, 2017) . Empirically, viable exclusion has been documented in taxonomically distant 114 

systems. A particularly clear case is the Cape ground squirrel (Waterman 2002) , in which 115 

matrilineal kin groups maintain a stable cap of approximately three breeding females through 116 

coordinated reproductive suppression of younger females, with fission occurring only when this 117 

active regulatory mechanism is overwhelmed by group growth — an empirical instance of viable 118 

exclusion holding group size below the size at which fission would otherwise be triggered. A 119 

second clear case is the clown anemonefish (Buston 2003) , in which dominant breeders regulate 120 

group size through forcible eviction of existing subordinates and active prevention of new 121 

recruitment, demonstrating viable exclusion in a phylogenetically distant taxon and through a 122 

different behavioral mechanism. But exclusion is costly, imperfect, and subject to free -riding 123 

among incumbents — conditions that limit how reliably it can hold group size at or below N* .124 

What the insider -outsider conflict framework does not address is the possibility that 125 

suboptimal modal group size could arise without exclusion at all, as a consequence of fission 126 

dynamics interacting with joiner pool ecology. This gap is one of the motivations for the present 127 

paper; we take it up alongside several additional problems that have remained unresolved in the 128 

joint analysis of admission, exclusion, and fission. 129 

Fission as a regulatory mechanism: what the literature has and has not addressed 130 

Fission, the splitting of an oversized group into two daughter groups, is recognized as the primary 131 

mechanism by which groups regulate upper size bounds in many taxa (Aureli, Schaffner, Boesch, 132 

Bearder, Call, Chapman, Connor, Fiore, Dunbar, Henzi, Holekamp, Korstjens, Layton, Lee, 133 

Lehmann, Manson, Ramos‐Fernandez, et al. 2008) . Seno (2006) modeled group size dynamics via 134 

fission and fusion with inclusive fitness considerations, recovering several of the qualitative 135 

predictions of Sibly’s framework. The broader fission -fusion literature has examined the 136 

behavioral and ecological triggers of splitting events and the consequences of fission for social 137 

structure (Sandel et al. 2026; Feder et al. 2025) .138 

A particularly direct theoretical precursor to the present framework is Smith’s (1985) 139 

analysis of Inuit foraging groups, which formalized the conflict of interest between current group 140 

members and prospective joiners. Smith showed that where the alternative to group membership 141 

is low -return solitary foraging, joiners prefer group sizes that exceed the per -capita optimum for 142 

incumbent members, with the conflict resolved in favor of joiner preferences in the absence of 143 

effective exclusion. We build on this foundation in two directions: by treating fission as an 144 5

additional regulatory mechanism that responds to dilution beyond the joiner -admission decision, 145 

and by promoting exclusion from a possibility weighed against admission to an active decision 146 

subject to coordination costs and free -riding. We also formalize how joiner pool structure (i.e., 147 

whether prospective joiners can compare available groups or encounter them sequentially) 148 

interacts with the conflict to determine the magnitude and direction of deviation from the optimum. 149 

Smith's relatedness weighting of payoffs is a natural extension within the inclusive -fitness framing 150 

adopted here: when r > 0, incumbents partially internalize fitness benefits flowing to related group 151 

members and related prospective joiners, raising the effective payoff of admission and attenuating 152 

the insider -outsider conflict that drives the present model's dynamics. The baseline analyses below 153 

treat r = 0 to isolate the structural results, but the directional prediction is clear — higher within -154 

group relatedness pushes modal group size upward relative to the r = 0 baseline. 155 

A separate body of theory addresses group -size distributions from the opposite direction, 156 

specifying rates of splitting and merging directly at the population level rather than deriving them 157 

from individual decisions. Lerch and Abbott (2024) and the coagulation -fragmentation tradition 158 

(Ma et al. 2011) are complementary in scope here, both characterizing population -level group -size 159 

distributions from specified between -group rates. Safran et al. (2007) identified this 160 

individual/population separation as a major gap in the group -living literature and called for models 161 

that link the two; the present framework supplies that link, using individual admit -exclude -fission 162 

decisions to predict the population -level signatures these distributional approaches characterize. 163 

What the prior literature has not examined is the population -level distribution of group 164 

sizes that emerges when fission interacts with different joiner ecologies. Earlier theoretical work, 165 

including Sibly's original analysis, implicitly assumed that each group attracts joiners from a local 166 

catchment independently of other groups. Under this Case 1 assumption (which we examine first 167 

below before relaxing), post -fission daughters each grow autonomously and the population -level 168 

modal size reflects the fission -regrowth cycle of individual groups: above N* for most of the cycle, 169 

settling near the fission threshold. This is the ecologically appropriate model for relatively isolated 170 

groups or for species in which prospective joiners encounter single groups rather than assessing 171 

multiple options simultaneously; Cases 2 and 3 below relax that assumption. 172 

## The fission -only model 173 

Setup 174 

The fission -only model of group size regulation allows us to ask what determines modal group 175 

size when fission is the only available regulatory mechanism (i.e., exclusion is not viable). The 176 

declining slope of the payoff function, captured by the parameter β introduced below, already 177 

embeds the ambient costs of group life (e.g., within -group competition, conflict, crowding, and 178 

disease) as passive costs that accumulate automatically with group size. The fission -and -exclusion 179 

model builds on this fission -only foundation by asking what happens when groups can actively 180 6

respond to those costs through exclusion, treating membership regulation as a strategic collective -181 

action problem rather than a fixed environmental parameter, while fission remains governed by 182 

the threshold and cost parameters established in Part 1. 183 

The payoff function 184 

Let R1 denote the per -capita return available to a solitary individual — the outside option against 185 

which group membership is evaluated. Let PI(n) denote the per -capita payoff of an intact group of 186 

size n. We model PI(n) as a piecewise linear function consisting of two line segments meeting at 187 

the nominal optimum N* : a rising segment below N* with slope α, and a declining segment above 188 

N* with slope β:189 

PI(n) = R1 + α(n − 1) for n ≤ N* 190 

PI(n) = Pmax − β(n − N* ) for n > N* 191 

where Pmax = R1 + α(N* − 1) is the peak per -capita payoff at the nominal optimum. The 192 

two slopes α and β are independent positive parameters. When α = β the function is symmetric, we 193 

get the classic tent shape used in most prior treatments. When α ≠ β the function is asymmetric: 194 

the rate at which membership becomes more valuable as the group grows toward N* differs from 195 

the rate at which it becomes less valuable as the group grows beyond it. Both slopes are empirically 196 

meaningful and, as we show below, their ratio determines the direction of modal group size 197 

deviation from N* under local joiner pool conditions. The piecewise linear form is adopted for 198 

analytical tractability; qualitatively identical results hold for smooth, continuously differentiable 199 

payoff functions with the same slope asymmetry. 200 

The two slopes capture a distinction that the symmetric model conceals: the processes that 201 

make groups valuable as they grow need not mirror the processes that make them costly as they 202 

grow further. On the rising side, benefits such as shared vigilance, cooperative foraging, and 203 

collective defense accumulate as new members contribute to public goods. On the declining side, 204 

costs such as within -group competition, conflict, crowding, and disease transmission accumulate 205 

as the group exceeds the size at which resources and social tolerance are comfortably shared. There 206 

is no theoretical reason these should be symmetric, and in practice they rarely are. 207 

Sources of payoff asymmetry: why α and β differ 208 

Understanding what drives slope asymmetry matters because, as the fission threshold derivation 209 

below shows, the ratio α/β determines the direction of modal group size deviation in a local joiner 210 

pool system. Giraldeau and Gillis (1985) first noted that slope asymmetry governs the direction of 211 

deviation from N*. We make their qualitative insight quantitatively precise by deriving the 212 

analytical relationship between α, β, and modal group size. Two broad classes of asymmetry 213 

generate the two directions of deviation; each is illustrated by familiar empirical systems. 214 7

Steep decline, shallow rise ( β > α): costs escalate faster than benefits accumulate. 215 

Several well -documented mechanisms generate the β > α asymmetry: density -dependent pathogen 216 

and parasite transmission, whose contact rates scale roughly with the square of group size (Altizer 217 

et al. 2003) ; intensifying within -group competition for rank and resources, with measurable fitness 218 

costs (slower infant development, longer interbirth intervals) in larger primate groups (Borries et 219 

al. 2008) ; and range -mediated predation exposure, as in Cape ground squirrels, where juveniles 220 

foraging farther from the refuge burrow suffer disproportionate mortality (Waterman 2002) . In 221 

each, the payoff function falls more steeply than it rises and the fission threshold is reached sooner 222 

than the symmetric model predicts. 223 

Shallow decline, steep rise ( α > β): benefits accumulate faster than costs escalate. The 224 

opposite asymmetry , α > β, arises where early members add large marginal benefits that saturate 225 

quickly while crowding costs stay flat across a wide supraoptimal range — as in cooperative 226 

predator defense, where each additional defender matters most at small group sizes (McNamara 227 

and Houston 1992) , and cooperative breeding, where scarce helpers contribute disproportionately 228 

to reproduction (Grinsted and Field 2018; Sturrock et al. 2022) . This raises the fission threshold 229 

above the symmetric baseline, so groups grow larger before splitting. 230 

How α/β maps onto conflict 231 

The α and β parameters have a natural interpretation in terms of two related conflicts that arise as 232 

groups grow. Below N*, adding a member benefits both incumbents and the newcomer at rate α ,233 

such that interests are aligned and there is no conflict. At N* and above, two conflicts emerge 234 

simultaneously and are both captured by β. The first is an insider -outsider conflict: adding a 235 

member benefits the joiner (whose alternative is solitary low -return existence) but harms each 236 

incumbent by β per added entry. The second is a within -group conflict: as the group exceeds N*, 237 

all current members face accumulating costs of crowding, competition for resources, social 238 

tension, and disease transmission, growing at the same rate β. The two conflicts are coupled, each 239 

new joiner intensifies within -group conflict for everyone present; but they are conceptually 240 

distinct, and the three mechanisms developed in this paper target them differently. 241 

The two models in this paper formalize three responses, distinguished by whether incumbents 242 

passively accept the resulting costs or actively bear a coordination cost to resolve them. Admission 243 

is a passive response to insider -outsider conflict: when n < N* , it realizes mutual benefit, and when 244 

n ≥ N*, it accommodates the joiner’s preference and lets each incumbent absorb the β cost rather 245 

than resolve it. Exclusion is the active counterpart: incumbents pay the per -capita coordination 246 

cost c + γN * to suppress insider -outsider conflict by enforcing their preference against further 247 

entry. Fission primarily targets within -group conflict, dissolving the shared social space when 248 

accumulated within -group costs exceed the per -capita cost of splitting; per -capita payoff recovers 249 

in each daughter, and within -group conflict is distributed between the now -smaller groups. The 250 

viable exclusion condition β > c + γN * identifies when exclusion is preferred over passive 251 8

accommodation; bringing the cost of fission into that comparison then determines when fission is 252 

preferable to either response. 253 

The joiner condition and why exclusion fails 254 

A prospective newcomer joins if and only if group membership yields a higher return than the 255 

outside option. This gives the joiner entry condition: 256 

join if PI(n + 1) > R1257 

On the rising side of the payoff function this condition is always satisfied for n < N* . On 258 

the declining side it remains satisfied until PI(n) returns to R1, which occurs at the Nash equilibrium 259 

size nNash = N* + ( Pmax – R1)/β – 1, one entrant below the size at which an incumbent’s payoff 260 

would itself fall to R1 (a joiner evaluates the post -entry payoff PI(n + 1), not PI(n)). In the 261 

symmetric case this is 2 N* − 1; with a steeper decline ( β > α) it is closer to N* ; with a shallower 262 

decline ( β < α) it is further above N* . Joining remains individually rational across this entire range. 263 

The admit option in the fission -and -exclusion model below adopts the same convention: the payoff 264 

to admitting one more member at size n is WA(n) = PI(n + 1), so the viable -exclusion comparison 265 

there is already evaluated post -entry. 266 

Incumbents prefer the group not grow beyond N* , since per -capita payoff declines above 267 

the nominal optimum. But any single incumbent considering resistance faces an asymmetric cost 268 

structure: exclusion costs (e.g., monitoring, signaling, aggression, coalition -building) fall privately 269 

on the excluder while the benefit of a less diluted group is shared by all. Exclusion is therefore a 270 

public good, undersupplied by individually rational actors. Without a mechanism for coordinating 271 

joint exclusion, the group cannot resist entry. The fission -and -exclusion model addresses what 272 

happens when such coordination becomes possible; in the fission -only model it does not exist. 273 

The fission threshold 274 

The fission threshold NF = N*·2( α+β)/( α+2 β) follows directly from solving PI(n/2) > PI(n) for the 275 

piecewise linear payoff function; the full derivation is given in Appendix A. The model treats 276 

fission as a symmetric split into two comparable daughters of roughly half the parent’s size. This 277 

is a tractable idealization at one end of a continuum; at the other end a single individual dissociates, 278 

sometimes drawing followers, to form a much smaller group that may later re -fuse. The threshold 279 

logic does not require the split to be exactly equal — asymmetric or partial fission alters the post -280 

split size distribution and how quickly the regrowth cycle resets, but not the existence of a size 281 

above which splitting becomes individually rational. 282 9

Fission costs and the realized fission threshold 292 

The fission threshold derived above assumes that splitting is costless. In practice, fission carries 293 

direct costs: groups must physically separate, often losing access to part of their territory; social 294 

bonds are disrupted; coalitional alliances formed within the parent group may not survive the split; 295 

and newly separated daughter groups face heightened intergroup threat during the period of 296 

reduced size. Let F denote the per -capita cost of fission, borne by all members of both daughters 297 

at the moment of splitting. The realized fission threshold NF(F) is the smallest group size at which 298 

the post -fission payoff net of fission costs first exceeds the intact -group payoff: 299 

NF(F) = min { n : PI(n/2) − F > PI(n) } 300 

Because fission costs reduce the post -fission payoff, they delay the point at which splitting 301 

becomes worthwhile. The realized threshold NF(F) therefore lies at or above the costless threshold 302 

NF: equal when F = 0, and strictly above for any F > 0, increasing monotonically with F. At F =303 10 

0, the realized threshold equals the payoff -optimal threshold derived above. As F increases, the 304 

realized threshold rises toward the Nash equilibrium size — and as fission costs become 305 

prohibitively large, the realized threshold reaches the Nash equilibrium and the group never 306 

fissions. This limiting case recovers Sibly’s (1983) result: groups grow to the Nash equilibrium 307 

size under open -access conditions and stabilize there, without any fission regulatory mechanism 308 

operating. 309 

Fission costs thus trace a continuum from the payoff -optimal threshold (costless fission) to 310 

the Nash equilibrium (prohibitively costly fission), with real systems occupying intermediate 311 

positions depending on the ecological and social costs of splitting. Notably, fission costs and slope 312 

asymmetry are independent parameters that can push in the same or opposite directions: a system 313 

with β > α (steep decline, low NF) and low fission costs may produce a lower realized threshold 314 

than a symmetric system with high fission costs, even though both have the same N* . The 315 

interaction of these two parameters determines the realized fission threshold and, through it, the 316 

time -averaged modal group size in a population. 317 

One clarification is essential for what follows. Throughout the fission -only model, F is 318 

treated as a fixed threshold parameter rather than a strategic decision variable. Groups do not 319 

choose whether to fission by actively weighing F against alternatives at each step; they fission 320 

when size crosses NF(F), with F determining where that threshold falls. This is a deliberate 321 

simplification that isolates the effect of fission costs on the group size cycle without requiring 322 

groups to make active cost comparisons. The fission -and -exclusion model, which follows, treats 323 

fission instead as an active decision that incumbent members must weigh against other options. 324 

There, fission enters as one of three genuine decision costs (alongside admitting a newcomer and 325 

paying to exclude one) and its strategic consequences become fully visible: when does excluding 326 

become cheaper than fissioning, and when does fissioning become the dominant response to a 327 

group that can no longer be cheaply regulated from within? These are the questions the fission -328 

and -exclusion model is built to answer. 329

With fission costs and slope asymmetry both in hand, we can now derive the time -averaged modal 331 

group size for a population of groups under local joiner pool conditions — the Case 1 regime in 332 

which each group draws from its own joiner stream independently. Under a constant joining rate 333 

of one new member per unit time, a group grows linearly from its post -fission starting size NF(F)/2 334 

to its realized fission threshold NF(F). The time -averaged group size over this cycle is the midpoint 335 

of the growth range: 336 

⟨n⟩ = (3/4) · NF(F)337 

This can be expressed in terms of the underlying parameters. For the baseline case with 338 

symmetric slopes and no fission costs, ⟨n⟩ = (3/4)(4/3) N* = N* , the time average coincides exactly 339 

with the optimum, even though groups range from 2/3 N* to 4/3 N* at any given moment. 340 11 

The direction of deviation from N* depends on the two parameters jointly. Fission costs 341 

always push ⟨n⟩ above N* : as F increases, NF(F) rises, and the entire growth cycle shifts upward. 342 

The group spends more time at supraoptimal sizes before the realized threshold triggers a split. 343 

Slope asymmetry pushes in a direction determined by the ratio α/β. When α > β (benefits 344 

accumulate faster than costs escalate), NF is high and the group spends more time above N* than 345 

below it: ⟨n⟩ > N* . When β > α (costs escalate faster than benefits accumulate), NF is low and the 346 

group spends more time below N* than above it: ⟨n⟩ < N* . These two parameters are additive in 347 

their directional effects on modal group size: when both push in the same direction — high fission 348 

costs together with α > β — modal size deviates strongly upward; when they oppose (e.g., steep 349 

decline but high fission costs), they partly counteract one another, with the dominant parameter 350 

determining the direction. 351 

The full expression for time -averaged modal size under costless fission and asymmetric 352 

slopes is: 353 

⟨n⟩ = (3/2) · N* · ( α + β) / ( α + 2 β)354 

This exceeds N* when α > β, equals N* when α = β, and falls below N* when β > α. The 355 

slope ratio α/β is therefore the key structural parameter governing modal group size in local joiner 356 

pool systems under costless fission, with fission costs providing an independent upward shift that 357 

applies regardless of slope asymmetry. 358 

Post -fission dynamics and the role of joiner pool structure 359 

The payoff parameters α, β, and F established above determine the mechanics of the fission cycle 360 

for any single group: when it splits, how large its daughters are, and how long the group spends 361 

above versus below N* before splitting again. Two aspects of fission matter here and recur below. 362 

The first is the size at which a group fissions , which the threshold conditions above treat as 363 

symmetric halving, the tractable baseline retained throughout for NF, NAF , and nEF . The second is 364 

its geometry: how the parent’s mass divides. Symmetric halving is only the special case; real 365 

fissions extend to the asymmetric, in which a small group dissociates from a crowded group , while 366 

the bulk persists. This too follows from the individual rationality that governs joining, once one 367 

notes that α and β are merely group averages: a group is not homogeneous but a set of individuals 368 

in shifting dyadic relationships, and the costs of crowding fall unevenly . As such , a member 369 

bearing a private cost well above the mean can rationally leave , alone if need be , even when the 370 

group -average payoff looks tolerable. We hold split geometry at symmetric halving for the 371 

threshold results below and let it vary when we turn to the population distribution, where it proves 372 

decisive. These parameters, however, say nothing about what happens to those daughters in a 373 

landscape that may contain other groups competing for the same prospective joiners. That question 374 

is governed by a second, independent dimension of the model: the structure of the joiner pool. 375 

These two dimensions (payoff parameters and joiner pool structure) interact to produce the full 376 

range of modal group size outcomes that the fission -only model predicts. The payoff parameters 377 12 

set the size and duration of the fission cycle; the joiner pool structure determines whether post -378 

fission daughters each run their own cycle autonomously or compete for the same joiners , and 379 

therefore whether the population distribution of group sizes is driven primarily by within -group 380 

fission -regrowth dynamics or by differential daughter growth across a shared landscape. We 381 

consider three cases, each internally consistent with the payoff assumptions above and each 382 

producing a qualitatively distinct population -level outcome. 383 

After fission at NF(F), two daughter groups exist, both starting at NF(F)/2 — below the 384 

nominal optimum and therefore attractive to prospective joiners under the entry condition. 385 

Case 1: Local joiner pools, independent growth. 386 

When prospective joiners arrive at each group independently from local catchments (e.g., 387 

because groups occupy separate territories, are rarely encountered simultaneously, or because 388 

joiners assess only one group at a time), post -fission daughters each draw from their own joiner 389 

streams. Both daughters grow autonomously and the population -level modal size is determined by 390 

the cycle -averaged formula derived above. Under symmetric slopes and costless fission, modal 391 

size is N* ; under asymmetric slopes or positive fission costs, it deviates in the directions described. 392 

The population at any snapshot contains groups distributed across the growth cycle from NF(F)/2 393 

to NF(F), with the time average as the central tendency. This is the appropriate model for relatively 394 

isolated groups or taxa in which prospective joiners encounter groups sequentially rather than 395 

comparatively. 396 

Case 2: Global joiner pool, asymmetric fission (the bimodal mechanism). 397 

When prospective joiners can assess and compare multiple groups across a shared 398 

landscape, Ideal Free Distribution logic applies: joiners prefer the group offering the highest post -399 

entry per -capita return. Under symmetric fission this does not strand the smaller daughter for long 400 

— joiners are pulled to whichever group is climbing toward the optimum, so both daughters pile 401 

up just below the fission threshold, leaving the standing distribution supra -optimal rather than sub -402 

optimal. Permanent stranding of daughter groups results only at the costless symmetric limit ( F =403 

0), where the unstranded, growing daughter reaches the fission threshold exactly as its post -entry 404 

payoff would fall to the smaller daughter — instructive, but biologically unexpected. The generic, 405 

robust route to sub -optimal sizes is asymmetric fission: more commonly, one or more members 406 

dissociate from a crowded group while the bulk persists. The per -capita payoff that defines 407 

crowding is a group average, but the underlying costs of conflict are not — a group is a set of 408 

individuals in shifting dyadic relationships, and a member bearing a private cost well above the 409 

mean does better by leaving, alone if need be, than by staying. A small daughter formed this way 410 

sits far below N* and is the least attractive option in the pool for joiners; it lingers at small size 411 

while large groups keep recruiting and drift to or beyond N*, producing a bimodal standing 412 

distribution: supra -optimal large groups coexisting with a sub -optimal mode of small daughters. 413 13 

Mechanically this is a coagulation –fragmentation balance (Ma et al. 2011; Lerch and 414 

Abbott 2024) : asymmetric fission continually produces small group fragment s while joining 415 

allows fragments to coagulate back into larger groups , so any one small group is transient even 416 

though the low group size mode it populates is permanent and continually refilled. Coordinating 417 

departure of a larger group is harder than dissociating alone — the fission -side analogue of the 418 

coordination cost that governs exclusion — so departures concentrate at the smallest sizes, and the 419 

height of the low mode is set by two observable quantities, the dissociation rate and the residence 420 

time of small groups , largely independent of slope asymmetry and fission cost, which instead 421 

govern the position of the supra -optimal mode as in Case 1 (Figure 2). The pattern reproduces the 422 

coexistence of large groups with lone individuals and small groups characteristic of fission -fusion 423 

societies  across taxa (Couzin and Laidre 2009) and well documented among primates  (Aureli, et 424 

al. 2008; Lehmann and Boesch 2004) .425 

Case 3: Global joiner pool, stochastic choice under weak discrimination. 426 

Joiners may be unable to reliably distinguish small payoff differences between available 427 

groups. After fission, daughters may differ only slightly in size and per -capita return; those 428 

differences may be too subtle to detect from a distance, too slow to manifest in observable cues, 429 

or too easily swamped by proximity and chance encounter. Where this is so, joining is probabilistic 430 

rather than strictly payoff -maximizing. Both daughters receive joiners but not in equal proportion: 431 

the larger daughter is more likely to be preferred in any given encounter, so it grows faster in 432 

expectation. The strength of this amplification depends on the ratio of payoff difference to 433 

assessment noise. When daughters are nearly equal, noise dominates and joiners distribute close 434 

to randomly; as size differences grow, joiner discrimination becomes more decisive and the 435 

dynamics approach Case 2. 436 

The Case 1 –2 continuum can be formalized with a logit choice rule in which a noise 437 

parameter σ indexes joiner discrimination precision; the full treatment including equilibrium 438 

dynamics and empirical estimation of 1/σ is given in Appendix D. 439 

Central result of the fission -only model 440 

The fission -only model maps modal group size onto two independent sets of parameters: the payoff 441 

function parameters ( α, β, and fission cost F) that govern the fission cycle, and the joiner pool 442 

structure (local versus global, and discrimination precision) that governs how post -fission 443 

daughters compete for joiners . The two sets interact to produce a parameter space with distinct 444 

regions corresponding to different modal group sizes. 445 

The key empirical implication is that the direction of modal group size deviation from N* 446 

is not fixed by group size theory but is system -specific, determined by identifiable ecological and 447 

behavioral parameters. Groups in systems dominated by rapidly escalating internal costs ( β > α)448 

and global joiner pools should show strong suboptimal modal sizes; groups in systems with slowly 449 14 

accumulating costs ( α > β), high fission costs, and local joiner pools should show supraoptimal 450 

modal sizes. The same payoff -function logic, the same individual rationality, and the same fission 451 

mechanism produce opposite directional predictions depending on parameters that are in principle 452 

observable. The result is a comparative empirical program: predict the direction in which any given 453 

population’s modal size deviates from its nominal optimum from a small set of ecological and 454 

behavioral parameters that can be measured in the field — α/β slope asymmetry, joiner pool 455 

structure, and fission cost. 456 

A further implication concerns within -population variance in group size. Case 1 predicts a 457 

unimodal distribution of group sizes across the fission -regrowth cycle, centered at ⟨n⟩ with spread 458 

determined by NF(F) – NF(F)/2. Case 2 predicts a bimodal distribution: a sub -optimal mode of 459 

small groups alongside a supra -optimal mode of large groups , with the small mode’s height set by 460 

the dissociation rate and group residence time. Case 3 predicts a broad, over -dispersed distribution 461 

spanning sub - and supra -optimal sizes. These distributional signatures are empirically 462 

distinguishable and provide a test of which regime a population is in, independently of the mean 463 

modal size. Figure 2 shows the three predicted signatures side by side. 464

## The fission -and -exclusion model and the viable exclusion condition 478 

Setup 479 15 

The fission -only model treated group maintenance as a passive cost environment: the declining 480 

slope β of the payoff function captured the ambient effects of within -group competition, conflict, 481 

crowding, and disease as costs that accumulate automatically with group size, regardless of what 482 

incumbents do. Within the fission -only model, fission cost F was also treated passively — as a 483 

threshold parameter that shifts the realized fission point NF(F), but not as a strategic decision 484 

weighed at each joiner arrival. The fission -and -exclusion model promotes both the cost of resisting 485 

unwanted joiners and the decision to fission from passive to active. Incumbent groups can now 486 

respond to dilution by attempting coordinated exclusion at a real per -capita cost, and the decision 487 

to fission is itself weighed against exclusion at every step rather than triggered automatically at a 488 

threshold. The model now contains three genuine decision costs operating at every joiner arrival: 489 

the cost of admitting (a per -capita payoff loss β at sizes above N* ), the cost of excluding ( CE(n), a 490 

coordination cost rising with group size), and the cost of fissioning ( F, the one -time per -capita 491 

penalty of splitting). Incumbents compare these three costs and act on the smallest. 492 

The three -decision framework 493 

When a prospective joiner arrives at a group of size n, the per -joiner decision is whether to admit 494 

or exclude that individual. Running underneath this binary choice is a group -level question that 495 

does not concern any single joiner: whether cumulative dilution has made splitting the whole group 496 

preferable to continuing under the better policy: admit or exclude. We write all three resulting 497 

payoffs on a common per -capita scale so that the two tiers can be compared directly, but they are 498 

not three interchangeable responses to one arrival — admit and exclude act on the joiner in front 499 

of the group, whereas fission is a threshold the group monitors as it grows: 500 

WA(n) = PI(n + 1) admit 501 

WE(n) = PI(n) − CE(n) exclude 502 

WF(n) = PI(n/2) − F fission 503 

Admit allows the group to grow by one member; all incumbents and the newcomer share 504 

the diluted payoff PI(n + 1). Exclude maintains size at n, but incumbents collectively bear the per -505 

capita cost CE(n) of organizing resistance. Fission splits the group equally into two daughters of 506 

size n/2 and imposes the per -capita fission cost F on every member. The optimal decision at any 507 

group size is whichever of WA, WE, and WF is largest. As n varies, the maximum can shift among 508 

the three options, partitioning the size range into zones of preferred action. Precisely , the model is 509 

a two -tier hierarchy. At each arrival, incumbents first resolve the per -joiner contest between 510 

admission and exclusion, comparing WA with WE; separately, they track whether growth has 511 

carried the group to the size at which fission first dominates the better of the two per -joiner options, 512 

i.e. whether WF exceeds max( WA, WE). That second comparison is a threshold test of exactly the 513 

form used in the fission -only model, with the admit -or -exclude winner replacing intact 514 

membership as the baseline. 515 16 

The decision rule is one -step and local: at each joiner arrival, the group evaluates the three 516 

payoffs at its current size and acts on the maximum. The cumulative trajectory of a group across 517 

time emerges from repeated application of this rule as joiners arrive. We assume a constant arrival 518 

rate of one prospective joiner per unit time, as in the fission -only model. 519 

Exclusion cost as a coordination problem 520 

Exclusion is a public good: when it succeeds, every incumbent enjoys the smaller, less diluted 521 

group, whether or not they helped achieve it. Exclusion requires effort (e.g., monitoring, signaling, 522 

aggression, or coalition maintenance ) that is often coordinated and borne privately by those who 523 

do the excluding . As group size grows, the number of incumbents whose must be coordinated 524 

around exclusion increases , and the temptation to free -ride on others’ effort grows 525 

correspondingly. The per -capita cost of exclusion rises with group size as a result. 526 

We capture this with a simple linear cost function: 527 

CE(n) = c + γn 528 

The γn term in CE(n) represents the expected cost of a coordination attempt that succeeds 529 

with a size -declining probability; the volunteer’s -dilemma micro -foundation is given in Appendix 530 

C. Together , c and γ specify how the second -order collective -action problem of exclusion translates 531 

into the cost paid by each willing excluder in a group of size n. Low c and low γ describe a system 532 

where coordination is cheap and free -riding is mild — small, well -organized groups or systems 533 

with clear sanctioning mechanisms. High c or high γ describe systems where coordination is costly 534 

or free -riding dominates as size grows. That larger groups secure collective goods less reliably, as 535 

individual willingness to contribute falls with size, is well -established (Archetti 2009) . The link 536 

between failed within -group coordination and fission is not a hypothetical construct: free -riding in 537 

coordinated exclusion is an experimentally documented driver of fission in human task groups 538 

(Hart and Van Vugt 2006) , where groups subject to within -group free -riding split along their pre -539 

existing internal divisions. 540 

The viable exclusion condition β > c + γN * follows from comparing WA(N* ) with 541 

WE(N* ); the derivation is given in Appendix B. Figure 3 plots WA, WE, and WF against group 542 

size for both regimes, showing the group locking at N* when exclusion is viable and fissioning 543 

at NAF when it is not. 544 17

Two regimes of modal group size 554 

The viability condition partitions parameter space into two regimes. Each yields a qualitatively 555 

distinct prediction about modal group size. 556 

Non -viable exclusion ( β < c + γN*).  When exclusion is too expensive to beat admission 557 

at N* , the group never excludes. Joiners are admitted past N* until fission becomes preferred over 558 

admission. The admit -fission crossing NAF is the smallest n at which WF > WA. Solving: 559 

NAF = 2 · [ N* (α + β) + F − β] / ( α + 2 β)560 

When F = 0, this is the costless fission threshold NF from the fission -only model, up to a 561 

small discrete -step correction (because admission moves the group by one whereas fission halves 562 

it). For positive F, NAF shifts upward as in the fission -only model. Within this regime, the 563 

introduction of exclusion as an option changes nothing: WE is never on the envelope, the policy is 564 

identical to the fission -only model’s admit -only -then -fission policy, and modal group size is the 565 

value averaged over the fission -regrowth cycle (weighted by how many joiner -arrivals the group 566 

experiences at each size). The fission -only model’s full case structure (i.e., local pools, global pool 567 

with preference, weak discrimination) applies unchanged. 568 18 

Viable exclusion ( β > c + γN *). When exclusion is sufficiently cheap, WE rises above WA569 

at N* . A group growing from below first encounters this crossing at n = N* : the joiner arriving 570 

when the group has just reached the nominal optimum is excluded rather than admitted. The group 571 

does not grow further. Under steady joiner arrival, the group remains at N* indefinitely, excluding 572 

every joiner that arrives. Modal group size in the viable regime is: 573 

⟨n⟩ = N* 574 

Fission never occurs in steady state. The group locks at N* before it has any opportunity to 575 

reach the fission threshold, and the fission decision is therefore never invoked under sequential 576 

growth dynamics. For completeness, the size at which exclusion would give way to fission if the 577 

group were pushed there by some external perturbation (e.g., demographic shock, environmental 578 

disturbance, or predation) is given by the exclude -fission crossing: 579 

nEF = 2 · [ N* (α + β) + F − c] / ( α + 2 β + 2 γ)580 

Under sequential growth from below N* , the group locks at N* and never approaches nEF .581 

The exclude -fission crossing therefore characterizes a hypothetical policy boundary rather than a 582 

realized group size, and matters only when groups are placed above N* by exogenous factors —583 

historical fission events that occurred before exclusion became viable, environmental shocks, or 584 

internal birth rates that outpace the rate at which exclusion can repel them. 585 

The sharp regime transition. The shift between regimes is discontinuous: infinitesimal 586 

changes in c, γ, or β across the boundary β = c + γN* qualitatively reorganize the population 587 

dynamics. This has no counterpart in the fission -only model, where modal size varies continuously 588 

with payoff parameters; here, viable exclusion creates a sharp boundary across which dynamics 589 

restructure abruptly. 590

Central result of the fission -and -exclusion model 609 

The fission -and -exclusion model maps modal group size onto five independent parameters: the 610 

payoff function parameters α and β (from the fission -only model), the fission cost F (from the 611 

fission -only model), the exclusion cost parameters c and γ (from the fission -and -exclusion model), 612 

and the joiner pool structure (also from the fission -only model). The five -parameter space is 613 

partitioned by a single inequality, the viable exclusion condition β > c + γN* , into two regimes 614 

with qualitatively distinct dynamics. 615 

Three empirically tractable predictions follow. First, the direction of modal group size 616 

deviation from N* is determined by the viability condition: systems satisfying β > c + γN* should 617 

show modal sizes at N* regardless of the joiner pool case, while systems violating it should show 618 

the case -specific deviations. Second, fission frequency drops to zero across the viability boundary: 619 

systems just inside the viable regime should show effectively no fission events in steady state, 620 

while systems just outside should show recurrent fission cycles. Third, the bimodal distribution of 621 

Case 2 persists in both regimes but its larger mode shifts from a cycling time -average (non -viable) 622 20 

to a pinned value at N* (viable), and the minting of new sub -optimal daughter s halts when the 623 

system enters the viable regime. 624 

The viability condition itself is empirically tractable. The slope β of the declining payoff 625 

function can be estimated from the rate at which per -capita measures of fitness, foraging success, 626 

or reproductive output fall as observed group size rises above the nominal optimum (Borries et al. 627 

2008) . The exclusion cost parameters c and γ can be estimated from behavioral measures of 628 

resistance effort and from the rate at which that effort fails as group size increases. Comparative 629 

tests across taxa or social systems can then map populations onto either side of the viability 630 

boundary and compare predicted to observed group size distributions. The framework provides 631 

not just a description of variation but a quantitative criterion for predicting it. 632 

## Discussion 633 

The framework's empirical reach spans the major taxonomic groups in which fission -fusion 634 

dynamics have been studied, including the skewed and often bimodal group -size distributions 635 

documented across many taxa (Couzin and Laidre 2009) . The fission -only model and the fission -636 

and -exclusion model together unify predictions that have been derived independently across fishes 637 

(Pitcher 1986; Buston 2003) , social insects (Grinsted and Field 2018a) , cooperatively breeding 638 

birds (Sturrock et al. 2022) , rodents (Waterman 2002) , primates (Borries et al. 2008; Markham et 639 

al. 2015; Wakeford and Cords 2025; Sandel et al. 2026a) , and human forager groups (Walker and 640 

Hill 2014) . We focus the comparative discussion that follows on chimpanzee ( Pan troglodytes )641 

and human forager cases, where the empirical literature on group -size dynamics is most developed 642 

and where we can directly examine the framework's predicted regime signatures. What unifies 643 

these cases methodologically is a single question, made tractable by the framework: how is the 644 

distribution of group sizes positioned and shaped relative to the nominal optimum, and why? Four 645 

measurable parameters do the work: the slope asymmetry α/β of the per -capita payoff function, 646 

the structure of the joiner pool, the per -capita cost of coordinated exclusion, and the fission cost. 647 

Each governs how readily one of the three modes of conflict resolution (admission, exclusion, and 648 

fission) becomes the dominant response in a given system. The se cases also set up a prediction the 649 

framework makes that no prior account does: that the paired daughters of a single fission should 650 

diverge systematically in how readily they admit newcomers, a comparative prediction we develop 651 

below .652 

Among wild primates, the puzzle of modal group sizes that deviate systematically from 653 

per -capita optima has remained an active concern of the field (Markham and Gesquiere 2017) .654 

Chimpanzees are a natural test case, because the size and composition of their parties deviate from 655 

per -capita optima and reorganize far more than food supply alone would predict — exactly the 656 

directional deviation the framework addresses. The framework treats two things as one process at 657 

different timescales: the everyday flux in which parties form, grow, shed members, and dissolve, 658 

and the rare permanent fission in which a whole community divides. The first is where the model's 659 21 

machinery operates directly; the second is its limiting case, reached when the same conflict, 660 

accumulated over years, hardens party boundaries into a community split . Food sets the stage for 661 

party size : its distribution governs where females range and how contestable access to them 662 

becomes (Pusey et al. 1997; Emery Thompson et al. 2007; Murray et al. 2007) , which is why party 663 

size responds more to how food is distributed than to its overall abundance, and why food's 664 

influence wanes where food is plentiful (Newton -Fisher et al. 2000; Lehmann and Boesch 2004) .665 

Protective benefits set the stage for community size: a larger community holds a larger territory 666 

and faces less pressure from neighbors, so between -group competition pushes community size 667 

upward and holds communities together above their nominal optimum (Lemoine et al. 2020; 668 

Wilson et al. 2014) . But what regulates party size on that stage is the conflict over mating access 669 

that the resulting distribution of estrous females generates: their presence draws larger parties even 670 

as the competition it provokes among males can drive some apart, so party size reflects how that 671 

conflict is mediated, moment to moment, through who is admitted, tolerated, and driven out 672 

(Goodall 1986). 673 

What our framework identifies is a balance between the conflict that accumulates within a 674 

group and the ease with which individuals resolve it by regrouping. The decisions the model 675 

formalizes are played out moment to moment at the party level, where individuals aggregate with 676 

a subgroup, are tolerated or not, and dissociate to join or form others. The base model's fission into 677 

comparable daughters is one end of a continuum; at its other extreme, a single individual 678 

dissociates, sometimes drawing followers, to form a smaller party that may later re -fuse, an 679 

asymmetric and impermanent fission (Aureli, et al. 2008) . Such dissociation is the joiner -pool 680 

dynamic running in real time: the flexible, arousal -sensitive party composition that field studies 681 

describe, featuring a male core whose membership shifts within and across days (Goodall 1986; 682 

Kawanaka 1984) . The cost that regulates it is borne by the dissociating individual who forfeits 683 

access to contested resources and is exposed to threats that a larger party buffers (Boesch 1991; 684 

Lehmann and Boesch 2004) . A daughter party formed this way can therefore inherit a lower per -685 

capita payoff than its parent. Community size sets how readily the party level reabsorbs an 686 

individual who dissociates: small communities sustain larger, longer -lasting parties with closer 687 

association between the sexes, while large communities sustain smaller, shorter -lasting parties 688 

with weaker association, and beyond some point these parties stop exchanging members (Lehmann 689 

and Boesch 2004) . Permanent community fission is the rare limiting case of this same process, 690 

when mating -access conflict intensifies faster than the party level can absorb it, which is why such 691 

splits are separated by roughly 500 years (Langergraber et al. 2014) . Ngogo, the largest community 692 

ever recorded at roughly 190 individuals, is the best -documented: association between two clusters 693 

progressively broke down until it split permanently into antagonistic factions (Sandel et al. 2026) .694 

Fission therefore forfeits this protective advantage: a daughter community holds a smaller territory 695 

and can, at the extreme, be destroyed by its neighbors, as the Kahama community was after 696 

splitting from Kasekela (Goodall 1986; Wilson et al. 2014) . The framework thus supplies a single 697 

account in which the admission, exclusion, and fission (dis sociation) decisions that shape a party 698 22 

from hour to hour are the same decisions that, accumulated and hardened, determine whether a 699 

community holds together or divides, wherever such decisions are made .700 

Smith's (1985) analysis of Inuit foragers was foundational in directing attention to conflict -701 

mediated gr oup size regulation . Since then, work across diverse forager populations has mapped 702 

how resource distribution, kinship structure, and sources of conflict shape group size and when 703 

groups fission, fuse, and reassemble (Hamilton et al. 2007; Hill et al. 2011) . Among these, kinship 704 

and protection determine where group size sits before conflict regulates the variation around it. 705 

Kinship works through relatedness: shared production, alliance, and cooperation accrue along kin 706 

lines, so that denser kin networks and shared production, effectively raising α relative to β, settle 707 

at larger optima (Walker 2014) ; ecological models predict these optima closely, as when Janssen 708 

and Hill (2014) derive an optimal 7 –8 hunters for the Ache. Protection works through numbers: 709 

intergroup lethal violence was a regular feature of mobile forager life and tracked resource scarcity 710 

(Allen et al. 2016; Glowacki 2025) , so where neighboring groups are hostile, the advantage of 711 

greater numbers holds communities together above their within -group optimum, the human 712 

parallel to the between -group force in chimpanzees. On these foundations the same conflict -713 

mediated regulation operates. Kinship sets the lines along which a community can divide, but what 714 

drives it to divide is conflict over mating and reproductive partners: when that conflict outgrows 715 

what the kinship and marriage alliances can contain, the community fissions, and it fissions along 716 

those kin lines. Walker and Hill's (2014) cross -cultural analysis bears this out, finding that most 717 

human group fissions are driven primarily by internal political conflict and only secondarily by 718 

resource scarcity, matching the prediction that fission resolves accumulated intra group conflict. 719 

The Yanomamö are the clearest case. Villages grow in part as raiding brings in women as captured 720 

wives and, across a generation, their offspring, even as that same concentration of contested 721 

reproductive partners intensifies the competition that kinship and marriage alliances must contain; 722 

when those alliances can no longer contain it, the village fissions along lineage lines (Chagnon 723 

1979) . Across these cases, what explains variation in group size around its optimum is neither 724 

kinship nor protection alone but the conflict -mediated regulation those forces give rise to: the same 725 

accumulat ed intra group conflict, resolved by fission, that the framework formalizes. 726 

The fission -only and fission -and -exclusion models combine to produce a prediction about 727 

post -fission behavior that neither part anticipates alone. After fission, paired daughters face 728 

asymmetric incentives regarding admission of newcomers. The larger daughter — closer to N*729 

and preferred by joiners — has reason to become selectively restrictive: unchecked growth will 730 

move it to the fission threshold, so the benefits of coordinating exclusion are real and immediate. 731 

The smaller daughter faces the opposite incentive structure: it is below N*, unattractive relative to 732 

its paired daughter, and liable to linger as a small, sub -optimal group . Admitting joiners freely —733 

lowering effective entry costs, tolerating individuals it might otherwise exclude — is its best 734 

available strategy for growing back toward the optimum. The two daughters should therefore 735 

diverge in admission behavior in the period immediately following fission, with the larger 736 

becoming more restrictive and the smaller more permissive, even when they were drawn from the 737 23 

same parent group under identical ecological conditions. The prediction is empirically tractable: 738 

recently fissioned daughter groups of similar ecological quality should differ in their tolerance of 739 

newcomers, with the smaller daughter showing systematically lower effective entry barriers 740 

following the split. To our knowledge, no existing theory produces this prediction and no empirical 741 

analysis tests for it; the framework therefore generates a tractable comparative question in fission -742 

fusion research. 743 

Several extensions are tractable but lie beyond the scope of this paper. The framework 744 

treats joiner arrival as a constant -rate process with one prospective joiner at a time. Where many 745 

joiners arrive simultaneously and compete for entry, the bargaining structure inverts: incumbent 746 

groups gain power over candidates, the cost of selectivity shifts onto applicants, and the 747 

appropriate model becomes a matching market with two -sided preferences (Gale and Shapley 748 

1962; Noë and Hammerstein 1994) . The framework also treats the joiner pool as homogeneous; in 749 

practice joiners vary along multiple dimensions — spatial proximity, kinship, age and sex 750 

composition, skill and knowledge, behavioral profile (Bowler and Benton 2005; Widdig et al. 751 

2006; Wakeford and Cords 2025; Schniter et al. 2015, 2018; Sih et al. 2004) — and daughter groups 752 

facing heterogeneous pools should differentiate to occupy distinct niches, a Hotelling -style 753 

dynamic that adds a second axis of post -fission divergence orthogonal to the size -based axis above. 754 

The relatedness extension introduced by Smith (1985) folds naturally into the framework as a 755 

reweighting of per -capita payoffs by within -group relatedness, though we have set that step aside 756 

here to isolate the structural results. The framework also defines a comparative test across the 757 

viable exclusion boundary that we do not run here: field estimation of the exclusion -cost 758 

parameters c and γ would place real systems on one side or the other, a tractable next step the 759 

model makes precise. Finally, the framework does not address how groups acquire the capacity 760 

for coordinated exclusion in the first place, nor does it treat fission as the extended social process 761 

documented in long -term studies of permanent splits (Feder et al. 2025; Sandel et al. 2026) ; these 762 

are also natural targets for future work. 763 

## Conclusion 764 

Observed group sizes are best understood not as failed approximations to a static optimum but as 765 

outcomes of conflict -mediated regulation through admission, exclusion, and fission decisions. 766 

Whether a group sits below, at, or above its nominal optimum depends on a small set of measurable 767 

parameters: the slope asymmetry of the per -capita payoff function, the structure of the joiner pool, 768 

and the cost of coordinated exclusion. A single inequality, the viable exclusion condition, separates 769 

two qualitatively distinct dynamic regimes, with implications for the modal group sizes observed 770 

across non -human animal groups, primate fission -fusion societies, and human foragers. The 771 

framework thus offers a way to read variation in group size across taxa as the systematic signature 772 

of underlying ecological and behavioral parameters rather than as evidence of optimization failure. 773
