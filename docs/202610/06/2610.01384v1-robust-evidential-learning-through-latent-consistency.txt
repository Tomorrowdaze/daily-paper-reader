ABSTRACT
Reliable uncertainty quantification is essential for deploying deep learning models
in high-stakes settings, where out-of-distribution and adversarial inputs can induce
confident but unreliable predictions. Evidential Deep Learning provides efficient
uncertainty estimates in a single forward pass, but can still assign high evidential
strength to inputs that are poorly supported by the learned representation, such
as adversarial inputs. We introduce CLEAR, a lightweight, task-agnostic post-
hoc method that improves evidential robustness without retraining or altering
the base prediction. Using held-out calibration data, CLEAR characterises the
group-conditioned geometry of the model’s latent space. At inference, it efficiently
generates perturbation views directly in the latent space and measures their conflict
relative to the calibrated geometry of the predicted group. High latent conflict
indicates unsupported evidence, which CLEAR uses to selectively reduce evidential
strength while retaining evidence for latent-consistent inputs. On ImageNet→CUB,
CLEAR improves OOD and adversarial AUROC by +8.29 and +5.01 while
running 17.4× faster than competing post-hoc methods while preserving predictive
performance across classification, regression, and object detection benchmarks.

INTRODUCTION
Deep learning has enabled increasingly capable predictive systems across domains such as computer
vision and natural language processing (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020; Achiam et al., 2023). However,
reliable deployment requires more than predictive accuracy. In safety-critical settings, models may
encounter out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs that differ from the data observed during training, or
adversarial inputs deliberately perturbed to induce erroneous predictions. In both cases, a model may
remain highly confident despite operating beyond the conditions or environment under which it has
been trained. Identifying such unreliable predictions is therefore fundamental to the safe deployment
of deep learning systems.
Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) provides a means of expressing how strongly a model’s prediction
should be trusted (Abdar et al., 2021). Approaches such as Bayesian neural networks (Goan & Fookes,
2020), Monte Carlo Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016), and conformal methods (Angelopoulos
et al., 2023) can provide reliable uncertainty estimates; however, they often require additional training
or repeated inference. Evidential methods offer an efficient alternative by estimating uncertainty
in a single deterministic forward pass. Evidential Deep Learning (EDL) models class probabilities
using a Dirichlet distribution (Sensoy et al., 2018), while Deep Evidential Regression (DER) uses a
Normal-Inverse-Gamma distribution for continuous predictions (Amini et al., 2020). Despite their
appeal, evidential models can still assign strong evidence to OOD and adversarial inputs for which
the prediction is poorly supported (Kopetzki et al., 2021).
Post-hoc methods have emerged as a particularly effective approach for improving uncertainty
robustness in evidential models, as they enhance uncertainty estimation without retraining the
predictor and have demonstrated strong robustness to OOD and adversarial inputs (Kopetzki et al.,
2021; Barker et al., 2026). However, existing approaches may require repeated model evaluations or

We introduce Conflict-aware Latent Evidence Adjustment for adversarial Robustness (CLEAR),
a task-agnostic post-hoc approach that evaluates whether the evidence assigned by a pretrained
evidential model is supported by its expected latent representation. Rather than repeatedly evaluating
transformed inputs through the full model, CLEAR first uses held-out ID data to calibrate the latent
geometry associated with different regions of the output space. At inference, CLEAR then generates
inexpensive perturbation views directly in latent space and measures their conflict relative to the
calibrated geometry of the predicted output group. Predictions exhibiting latent behaviour consistent
with the calibrated ID structure retain their evidential strength, whereas high latent conflict indicates
unsupported evidence and results in increased uncertainty. CLEAR therefore improves uncertainty
under OOD and adversarial inputs without retraining the predictor or replacing its base prediction.

RELATED WORK
Uncertainty Quantification. UQ characterises uncertainty arising from incomplete model knowledge
and variability in observations (He & Jiang, 2023). Input-dependent distributions capture aleatoric
uncertainty (Kendall & Gal, 2017), while Bayesian inference (Goan & Fookes, 2020; Blei et al.,
2017), Monte Carlo Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016), deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al.,
2017), and distance-aware models (Liu et al., 2020) address epistemic uncertainty. Ensembles
with prediction intervals can account for both sources (Pearce et al., 2018). Test-time augmentation
measures predictive variability without explicitly separating them (Ayhan & Berens, 2018). Conformal
methods provide calibrated sets or intervals whose cardinality or width can serve as uncertainty
indicators (Angelopoulos et al., 2023). These approaches differ in their computational and calibration
requirements, motivating efficient estimation of both uncertainty types.
Evidential Deep Learning.
Evidential deep learning (EDL) applies Dempster-Shafer theory
(DST) (Dempster, 1968) to model class probabilities with a Dirichlet distribution, enabling epistemic
and aleatoric uncertainty estimation in a single forward pass. Its full formulation is given in Section 3.
EDL has been widely used for OOD detection by assigning greater uncertainty to unexpected inputs.
However, its deterministic inference lacks the multiple predictive perspectives provided by methods
such as Monte Carlo Dropout or deep ensembles, making overconfident errors difficult to recover
from. This limitation is particularly evident under adversarial perturbations (Kopetzki et al., 2021).
Post-hoc EDL methods can improve the robustness of evidential models to adversarial and distribution-
shifted inputs by refining uncertainty after the base model has been trained (Barker et al., 2026;
Kopetzki et al., 2021). However, many approaches rely on multiple forward passes or stochastic
sampling, increasing computational cost and sacrificing one of EDL’s key advantages: single-pass
uncertainty estimation. Others are tailored to specific tasks, e.g., classification (Barker et al., 2026),
limiting their generality. These limitations motivate a lightweight post-hoc approach that strengthens
evidential uncertainty under challenging inputs while preserving the efficiency and task flexibility of
the underlying task.

CONFLICT-AWARE LATENT EVIDENCE ADJUSTMENT FOR ADVERSARIAL
ROBUSTNESS
Our Conflict-aware Latent Evidence Adjustment for adversarial Robustness (CLEAR) approach,
whose high-level workflow is shown in Figure 1, boosts uncertainty under OOD and adversarially
attacked inputs by determining whether a model’s evidential strength is supported by familiar ID
latent structure. During calibration, CLEAR characterises the latent structure of ID data from a
pretrained evidential model without modifying or retraining the predictor. At inference, the model
produces a latent representation h(x) and evidential output, from which CLEAR samples multiple
latent perturbation views and forms all unordered pairs between them. Their compatibility with
the calibrated latent geometry of the predicted group is then measured, and evidential strength is
down-weighted when conflict is high. Consequently, inputs inconsistent with the learned ID structure
receive greater uncertainty, while evidence for compatible ID inputs is preserved.

4.1
LATENT CALIBRATION
CLEAR first estimates the approximate latent geometry associated with each output group using
a held-out calibration set Dcal. The evidential model is frozen, and CLEAR extracts the latent
representation of each calibration example hi = h(xi). Each example is assigned to an output

where the target space is continuous, targets are partitioned into B bins and gi = b(yi) where b(·)
maps a target value to its corresponding output bin. These bins are formed using calibration-set
quantiles, giving approximately equal group cardinalities Ng.

then determines the output group ˆg associated with the base prediction.

RESULTS
We evaluate CLEAR in a comprehensive series of experiments comparing it against state-of-the-art
EDL-based and other competitive UQ approaches over 10 independent runs. Our evaluation focuses
on both performance and uncertainty estimates produced per approach for OOD and adversarially
attacked data.
Comparative Approaches. We compare CLEAR against Posterior Networks (Charpentier et al.,
2020), Evidential Deep Learning (EDL) (Sensoy et al., 2018), Fisher Information-based EDL (I-
EDL) (Deng et al., 2023), Hyper-Opinion EDL (H-EDL) (Qu et al., 2024), Relaxed EDL (R-
EDL) (Chen et al., 2024), Regularized Evidential Model (RED) (Pandey & Yu, 2023), Smoothed
EDL (S-EDL) (Kopetzki et al., 2021), and Conflict-aware Evidential Deep Learning (C-EDL) (Barker
et al., 2026) to represent a range of approaches in EDL and UQ that allow for a fair comparison.

5.1
CORE RESULTS
Table 1 shows that CLEAR provides consistently strong uncertainty performance across the core
classification benchmarks while preserving the predictive performance of the underlying evidential
model. This behaviour is also reflected in Figure 2, where CLEAR generally occupies the desirable
operating region across both OOD and adversarial objectives. On MNIST→FashionMNIST, CLEAR
achieves the highest ID coverage (94.83%), lowest OOD and adversarial coverage (1.84% and 7.21%),
and highest AUROC and AAUROC (98.54% and 97.66%). This performance largely persists across
the remaining MNIST settings: on MNIST→KMNIST, CLEAR achieves the highest ID coverage,
lowest OOD coverage, and highest AUROC, while retaining an AAUROC of 97.36%; on the near-
OOD MNIST→EMNIST setting, it again achieves the strongest ID/OOD coverage together with the
highest AUROC and AAUROC.
The advantage of CLEAR becomes particularly apparent on the more challenging CIFAR10 ex-
periments. On CIFAR10→SVHN, CLEAR achieves the lowest adversarial coverage (36.38%) and
highest AAUROC (77.45%), while on CIFAR10→CIFAR100 it again achieves the highest AAUROC
(71.93%) while remaining competitive in ID retention and OOD detection. Figure 2 shows that
7


CLEAR therefore maintains strong performance across both uncertainty objectives rather than favour-
ing one form of distribution shift. Cases where CLEAR is weaker on an individual metric, including
adversarial coverage on MNIST→KMNIST and OOD separation on the CIFAR10 benchmarks, are
examined in more detail in Appendix D.
This pattern continues on the larger and more difficult benchmarks. On Oxford Flowers→DeepWeeds,
CLEAR achieves the lowest non-degenerate OOD and adversarial coverage (5.09% and 6.77%) and
the highest AAUROC (95.60%), while retaining an AUROC of 95.57%. On ImageNet→CUB,
a substantially harder setting due to ImageNet’s large-scale 1000-class output space and highly
diverse visual representation, CLEAR achieves the highest AUROC and AAUROC, at 84.63% and
92.61%, while retaining 80.54% ID coverage. The degenerate operating points observed for several
comparative methods on these harder benchmarks are also discussed in Appendix D. Together, these
results show that CLEAR remains effective as dataset scale and difficulty increase.

5.2
REGRESSION RESULTS
Across the regression benchmarks, CLEAR consis-
tently improves separation between ID predictions
and both OOD and adversarial inputs while pre-
serving comparable predictive error. On Califor-
nia Housing, CLEAR reduces OOD coverage from
91.60% with DER to 26.34% and adversarial cov-
erage from 99.90% to 1.05%, while increasing AU-
ROC and AAUROC to 71.01% and 89.75%, respec-
tively. This is accompanied by lower ID coverage
(57.18%), a trade-off also observed more mildly on
several other regression benchmarks and discussed
further in Appendix D. Importantly, the underlying
regression prediction is unchanged, so the stronger
rejection does not correspond to degraded predic-
tive accuracy. Similar improvements are observed
on Wine Quality, where CLEAR achieves an AU-
ROC of 89.10% and AAUROC of 89.99% without
changing MAE or RMSE.
The Airfoil experiment provides an especially clear
example under covariate shift, where OOD samples
are defined by extreme values of the input frequency
variable rather than by extremes of the regression
target. This makes the shift difficult for uncertainty
estimates that do not adequately capture changes in
the input representation: DER and UR-ERN retain
0% of both ID and OOD samples under the selected threshold, indicating little useful separation
between the two distributions. In contrast, CLEAR retains 72.87% ID coverage while reducing OOD
and adversarial coverage to 20.46% and 0.62%, respectively, with an AAUROC of 94.79%. This
suggests that CLEAR’s latent-space conflict is sensitive to changes in the covariate distribution even
when the prediction target itself is not the variable used to define the shift.
This behaviour remains consistent on the UTKFace experiments. For UTKFace→Oxford Flowers,
CLEAR reduces OOD and adversarial coverage to 14.98% and 18.09%, while increasing AUROC
and AAUROC to 87.53% and 85.38%. The subtler UTKFace age-extremes shift is more competitive,
because the OOD samples remain within the same face domain and differ only in their target
distribution, resulting in less distinct separation between their latent representations and those of ID
samples. Consequently, CLEAR achieves an AUROC of 60.77% remaining close to the best value of
61.63%, while still improving adversarial coverage to 44.01% and AAUROC to 53.32%. Overall,
8


these results show that CLEAR generalises its uncertainty improvements beyond classification,
maintaining strong predictive performance while improving robustness to both distribution shift and
adversarial perturbation.

OBJECT DETECTION RESULTS

CLEAR also generalises effectively to object detection,
where uncertainty must reflect not only whether a detec-
tion is reliable but also the quality of its localisation. As
shown in Table 7, Appendix C.6, CLEAR improves every
evaluated uncertainty metric over EMM on clean CelebA
detections, increasing AUROC from 50.3% to 58.5% and
AUPRC from 12.5% to 21.1%, while reducing FPR95
from 90.7% to 82.4% and ECE from 84.4% to 75.0%. Im-
portantly, the correlation between uncertainty and localisa-
tion error increases from approximately zero (r = 0.003)
for EMM to r = 0.168 for CLEAR, indicating that poorly
localised detections are more consistently assigned greater
uncertainty. This is important for object detection, where
a confident detection does not necessarily imply that its
predicted bounding box is accurately localised.
This localisation-aware behaviour persists under covariate
corruption. Table 8, Appendix C.6, shows that across
brightness, blur, noise, compression, and resize/crop shifts,
CLEAR produces a stronger positive association between
predictive uncertainty and localisation error than EMM in
every case, increasing the overall Pearson correlation from
0.010 to 0.049. Although the absolute correlations remain
modest, the consistent improvement across all five shifts
indicates that CLEAR better reflects degradation in bounding-box quality as the input distribution
changes. This trend is further illustrated in Figure 9, where CLEAR maintains a stronger uncertainty-
localisation relationship across the evaluated corruptions. Together, these results demonstrate that
CLEAR’s latent-conflict mechanism transfers beyond classification and regression to structured
localisation outputs while producing uncertainty that is more informative of detection quality. Full
training details for this setup are described in Appendix E.2.

FURTHER EXPERIMENTS AND ABLATION
An extensive set of additional experiments is provided in the Appendix, including corrupted bench-
marks (Appendix C.1) and additional uncertainty metrics (Appendix C.2). We then examine CLEAR
through hyperparameter ablations (Appendix C.3), uncertainty-threshold analysis (Appendix C.4),
adversarial attack analysis (Appendix C.5), and extended object-detection results (Appendix C.6).
Finally, we investigate the latent mechanism through held-out validation of the calibrated geometry
(Appendix C.7), sensitivity to latent representation depth (Appendix C.8), and qualitative visualisation
of the generated latent perturbation views (Appendix C.9). Across these analyses, CLEAR maintains
strong uncertainty performance under corruption and increasing attack strength, remains broadly
stable across its hyperparameters and thresholding method, and exhibits consistent behaviour across
different latent representations.
6
CONCLUSIONS
We introduced CLEAR, a lightweight post-hoc method for improving evidential uncertainty without
retraining or altering the underlying prediction. Across a broad set of datasets spanning classifi-
cation, regression, and object detection, CLEAR consistently improves uncertainty quality while
preserving predictive performance. A thorough evaluation across diverse OOD settings, covariate
shifts, adversarial attacks, and uncertainty thresholds demonstrates that CLEAR remains effective
under substantially different forms of distributional and adversarial challenge. Importantly, CLEAR
9


improves complementary uncertainty objectives jointly, avoiding the pronounced trade-offs exhibited
by several comparative methods, while introducing only modest additional inference overhead. These
results demonstrate that latent conflict provides a practical and broadly applicable signal for identify-
ing unsupported evidential confidence. Future work will investigate evaluating CLEAR on denser
structured prediction tasks, such as semantic segmentation, where uncertainty must be estimated over
spatially distributed outputs.

A
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Algorithms 1 and 2 summarise the calibration and inference stages of CLEAR. The pretrained
evidential model remains frozen and in evaluation mode throughout both stages. Calibration is
performed once, after which the group statistics and global reference conflict are reused for subsequent
inputs.
During calibration, class labels or target-quantile bins determine the groups used to estimate µg, Lg,
and ρg. Reference conflict is then evaluated relative to the group ˆg selected by each example’s original
prediction, matching the inference procedure. Each calibration example receives T independent
perturbation draws, each containing V latent views. The raw conflict scores are averaged per example
before taking the median across Dcal to obtain r.
At inference, a single forward pass produces the latent representation and original evidential output.
Each input then receives one set of V latent views, with no additional evaluations of the backbone
or evidential head. Whitening is implemented using triangular solves with the stored Cholesky
factor Lˆg. Pairwise conflict is computed using vector reductions, avoiding explicit construction of
cross-dimensional interaction matrices. The resulting Craw(x) is mapped to C(x) and used to obtain
the evidence scaling factor s(x). The final adjustment preserves the predicted class in classification
and the predictive mean γ(x) in regression.

C.2
EXTRA METRICS ANALYSIS
Table 4 provides additional insight into how CLEAR modifies the uncertainty produced by the
underlying evidential model. Across the MNIST and CIFAR settings, CLEAR produces consistently
larger confidence reductions for OOD and adversarial inputs, supporting the intended behaviour of
the method: latent conflict primarily acts on predictions whose representations are inconsistent with
the calibrated ID geometry, rather than uniformly suppressing evidence. This effect is strongest on
settings where ID and shifted inputs are well separated in latent space, while the smaller confidence
drops observed on the more challenging Oxford Flowers and ImageNet benchmarks suggest that
the distinction becomes less pronounced when the underlying representation itself provides weaker
separation. NLL and ECE show a different behaviour because these metrics evaluate calibration of the
ID predictive distribution rather than separation between ID and unreliable inputs. CLEAR does not
explicitly optimise either quantity and, by reducing evidential strength for latent-conflicting samples
while preserving the base prediction, can produce more conservative confidence estimates without
necessarily improving conventional calibration metrics. The results therefore highlight the distinction
between predictive calibration and uncertainty separation: CLEAR is designed to expose unsupported
evidence under distribution shift and attack, with its strongest gains appearing in the confidence
separation between reliable and unreliable inputs. The additional latent conflict computation also
introduces only a modest inference overhead relative to the underlying single-pass models; this
computational behaviour is examined further in Section E.7.
C.3
ABLATION ANALYSIS
We assess the sensitivity of CLEAR to five quantities controlling its latent conflict mechanism: the
number of latent views V , perturbation magnitude τ, evidence down-weighting strength δ, reference
conflict quantile q, and, for regression, the number of output bins B. Unless otherwise stated, we
use V = 3, τ = 0.05, δ = 10, and q = 0.5, with all non-ablated parameters held fixed. The first
four parameters affect different stages of the same inference process: V determines how many local
latent views are sampled, τ controls their displacement from the original representation, q determines
the calibration reference against which raw conflict is compared, and δ controls how strongly the
resulting conflict reduces evidential strength. The regression-specific parameter B instead determines
the granularity of the output-conditioned latent geometry.
Figure 4 examines the perturbation magnitude τ used to generate latent views, hv = h + τρˆgηv.
Performance is remarkably stable across the evaluated range. ID, OOD, and adversarial coverage and
both AUROC measures change only marginally for most datasets, despite the substantial change in
the absolute perturbation scale. This robustness is consistent with the calibration-relative construction
of CLEAR: τ changes the neighbourhood explored around the latent representation, but conflict is
subsequently interpreted relative to a reference estimated using the same perturbation mechanism.

C.4
THRESHOLD ANALYSIS
Table 5 examines the effect of the uncertainty measure used for thresholding. CLEAR performs
consistently across all four measures, maintaining strong OOD and adversarial separation despite
changes in the uncertainty statistic. Differential entropy provides the strongest overall balance,
retaining 94.83% of ID samples while reducing OOD and adversarial coverage to 1.84% and 7.21%,
with ID-OOD AUROC of 98.54% and ID-Adv. AUROC of 97.66%.
The alternative measures remain competitive, particularly for adversarial detection. Predictive entropy
achieves an AAUROC of 95.36% with adversarial coverage of 4.61%, while mutual information
and total evidence retain AAUROC above 93%. Although these alternatives reduce ID coverage and
OOD separation relative to differential entropy, CLEAR continues to distinguish unreliable inputs
effectively across all settings. This indicates that the effectiveness of the latent conflict adjustment
is not tied to a particular uncertainty statistic, while differential entropy offers the most favourable
balance between retaining ID predictions and rejecting OOD and adversarial inputs.
The comparative approaches show greater variation across threshold types, with changes in the
uncertainty statistic often producing larger shifts in coverage and AUROC. CLEAR therefore exhibits
comparatively stable behaviour across the evaluated thresholding choices, while differential entropy
provides a consistent default operating point for the main experiments.

ADVERSARIAL ATTACK ANALYSIS
Table 6 evaluates robustness across multiple adversarial attacks and perturbation strengths. CLEAR
performs consistently strongly across the evaluated settings, achieving the lowest adversarial coverage
in all but one case and the highest AAUROC in all but the weakest L2-PGD setting, where it effectively
matches PostNet (98.93% versus 98.98%). Its advantage becomes more pronounced as gradient-
based attacks strengthen. Under L2-PGD at strength 1.0, CLEAR retains adversarial coverage of only
7.21% and AAUROC of 97.66%, while several alternatives exhibit substantially greater degradation.
A similar pattern is observed for FGSM, where CLEAR achieves near-zero adversarial coverage at
strength 1.0 while maintaining an AAUROC of 97.88%.
CLEAR is also particularly strong under AutoAttack, achieving the highest AAUROC at all three
perturbation strengths: 98.87%, 98.35%, and 96.14%. At the strongest setting, its adversarial
coverage remains low at 23.79%, only slightly above H-EDL at 21.56%, while CLEAR maintains a
substantially higher AAUROC of 96.1% compared with 88.45%. Thus, despite the small difference
in coverage, CLEAR provides markedly stronger overall separation between clean and adversarial
inputs. For Salt and Pepper corruption, CLEAR also achieves the lowest coverage and highest
AAUROC across all three strengths, reaching 1.28% coverage and 98.63% AAUROC at strength 1.0.
Overall, CLEAR maintains strong performance across qualitatively different attack mechanisms and
degrades substantially less than most comparative approaches as adversarial strength increases.
C.6
FULL OBJECT DETECTION RESULTS
To evaluate CLEAR beyond classification and regression, we additionally consider object detection
on CelebA using YOLO11n (Khanam & Hussain, 2024). Object detection provides a useful test case
because uncertainty should reflect not only whether a detection is correct, but also how accurately its
bounding box is localised.
We use YOLO11n as a fixed base detector and train an Evidential Meta Model (EMM) (Shen et al.,
2023) on its intermediate features to produce detection-level evidential uncertainty over bounding-box
localisation. EMM is well suited to this setting because it adds an evidential uncertainty model on top
of a pretrained detector without requiring the detector itself to be modified. This allows YOLO11n to
remain unchanged while providing the evidential outputs required by CLEAR. Otherwise, YOLO11n
either cannot provide preliminary uncertainty outputs for CLEAR to build upon or would require full
retraining if the head is replaced with a Dirichlet head, which would be extremely computationally
expensive.

C.8
SENSITIVITY TO LATENT REPRESENTATION DEPTH
CLEAR uses the penultimate representation by default, as this is the final task-relevant feature
representation provided to the evidential head. To assess whether its performance depends on this
particular choice, we additionally evaluate CLEAR using representations extracted at progressively
deeper stages of the same ResNet-18 backbone. Specifically, we consider the outputs of residual
layers 1-3 and the penultimate representation, corresponding to latent dimensionalities of 64, 128, 256,
and 512, respectively. For each of 10 runs on MNIST→FashionMNIST, the underlying evidential
model is trained only once and then held fixed across all four settings.
Table 10 shows that CLEAR remains highly effective across all evaluated representation depths. As
expected, ID accuracy is identical across layers because the frozen predictive model is unchanged. The
earlier representations perform particularly strongly on this benchmark: layer 1 achieves the highest
ID coverage of 98.98% and lowest OOD coverage of 0.41%, while layer 2 achieves the highest
ID-OOD AUROC of 99.91% and AAUROC of 99.93%. The penultimate representation nevertheless
retains strong separation, with AUROC and AAUROC of 98.42% and 98.29%, respectively.
Importantly, these results show that the penultimate representation used throughout the main ex-
periments was not selected through benchmark-specific layer optimisation; indeed, shallower rep-
resentations perform better for this particular dataset pair. We retain the penultimate layer as the
default because it provides the representation directly consumed by the evidential head and gives a
consistent, architecture-independent definition of the latent space across the different models and
tasks considered in this work. The results further indicate that alternative intermediate representations
could provide additional gains when architecture-specific layer selection is appropriate.

C.9
QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS OF LATENT PERTURBATION VIEWS
Figure 11 provides an input-space interpretation of the latent perturbations used by CLEAR. For
each input, two latent views are sampled using the predicted group’s calibrated covariance geometry,
following Equation 11. To visualise these otherwise high-dimensional representations, we perform
feature inversion by optimising a copy of the input image such that its representation under the
frozen encoder approaches the corresponding target latent view, while applying pixel-distance and
total-variation regularisation to retain an interpretable image. Across the Oxford Flowers examples,
the resulting feature-inverted views remain visually close to the corresponding input, preserving the
dominant flower structure, colour, and semantic content. The DeepWeeds examples similarly retain
their overall appearance, showing that the perturbations remain local rather than moving to arbitrary
regions of the representation space. Importantly, however, their latent behaviour differs substantially
when evaluated relative to the calibrated Flowers geometry. For the examples shown, raw conflict
ranges from 54.51 to 323.75 for ID inputs, compared with 976.11 to 2459.20 for OOD inputs. Thus,
even when the perturbed views remain visually plausible, CLEAR can identify substantially greater
incompatibility with the latent structure expected for the predicted group.
This distinction illustrates that CLEAR’s conflict score does not simply measure the visible magnitude
of a perturbation. Instead, it captures the cross-dimensional behaviour of the perturbed representations
after conditioning on the calibrated group geometry. The qualitative examples therefore provide an
intuitive view of how locally similar latent perturbations can remain compatible for familiar ID inputs
but produce substantially greater conflict for unsupported OOD representations. Feature inversion
is used here only as an interpretive visualisation and is not part of CLEAR inference. Moreover,
the perturbation magnitude is deliberately enlarged for this analysis to make the corresponding
image-space changes perceptible; the absolute conflict values shown should therefore be interpreted
qualitatively rather than as reproductions of the operating scores used in the main experiments.
D
ADDITIONAL DISCUSSION
Trade-offs between OOD and adversarial uncertainty.
Some comparative approaches achieve
stronger individual metrics than CLEAR, but Figure 2 shows that these gains are often accompanied
by considerably larger losses on complementary uncertainty objectives. Importantly, the trade-off is
frequently asymmetric: when CLEAR does not rank first, it generally remains close to the strongest
result, whereas methods that outperform CLEAR on one metric can fall substantially behind on
another. On MNIST→KMNIST, for example, CLEAR’s AAUROC of 97.36% is only 0.68 percentage
points below H-EDL and 0.15 below C-EDL, while still achieving the strongest ID coverage, OOD
coverage, and ID-OOD AUROC. The contrast is more pronounced on CIFAR10→CIFAR100, where
CLEAR is only 1.76 points below the best OOD AUROC and 4.41 points above the best adversarial
coverage, yet achieves an AAUROC of 71.93%, compared with only 12.22% for C-EDL, the method
attaining the highest OOD AUROC. Figure 2 therefore illustrates that CLEAR’s occasional losses tend
to be comparatively small, while the complementary performance sacrificed by the corresponding best-
performing alternatives can be substantially larger. This motivates evaluating uncertainty methods
jointly across ID retention, OOD detection, and adversarial detection rather than selecting them
according to a single favourable metric.
Behaviour on the more challenging benchmarks.
The Oxford Flowers and ImageNet experiments
expose several operating regimes that are less apparent on the smaller benchmarks. On Oxford
Flowers→DeepWeeds, R-EDL achieves the highest OOD AUROC, but its adversarial separation is
considerably weaker than CLEAR. PostNet reports an adversarial coverage close to zero, but this
accompanies an ID accuracy of only 5.92% and ID coverage of 14.30%, indicating degradation of the
underlying predictor rather than useful selective uncertainty. The low-shot nature of Oxford Flowers
likely makes class-conditioned density estimation particularly difficult, highlighting the importance
of interpreting coverage together with predictive performance.
On ImageNet→CUB, the much larger 1000-class output space produces more pronounced extremes.
I-EDL accepts almost all ID and OOD samples, whereas S-EDL and C-EDL reject almost all samples,
including ID inputs. These behaviours likely reflect increased sensitivity of their uncertainty mecha-
nisms at this scale: the substantially larger and more heterogeneous representation and output spaces
can alter the magnitude and distribution of evidential, smoothing, and conflict-based uncertainty
when method-specific settings are transferred unchanged from smaller benchmarks. These operating
27


points can therefore produce favourable values for individual coverage metrics while providing
limited practical selectivity. We deliberately retain common method-specific settings across datasets
rather than independently tuning each approach for every benchmark, so these results also reveal
differences in how the methods transfer to larger-scale settings. CLEAR avoids either extreme,
retaining substantial ID coverage while achieving the highest OOD and adversarial AUROC.
Applying CLEAR to conventional pretrained models.
A practical limitation of CLEAR is that it
requires an evidential model from which both a latent representation and evidential output can be
obtained. Consequently, when only a conventional pretrained model is available, directly applying
CLEAR may otherwise require retraining the predictor using an evidential objective and evidential
head, which can be computationally prohibitive for large models or datasets. A lightweight alternative
is to use an Evidential Meta Model (EMM) (Shen et al., 2023), which learns an evidential output
from intermediate representations of a frozen pretrained model. This allows the original predictor
to remain unchanged while providing the evidential representation required by CLEAR. Depending
on the task, such a meta-model can parameterise an appropriate evidential distribution, such as a
Dirichlet distribution for classification or a Normal-Inverse-Gamma distribution for regression. Our
object-detection experiments, seen in Appendix C.6, demonstrate this strategy in practice: EMM is
trained over frozen YOLO11n features to model bounding-box localisation uncertainty, after which
CLEAR is applied post-hoc to the resulting evidential model. The resulting improvements over EMM
alone show that CLEAR can therefore be used with existing pretrained predictors without requiring
the underlying model itself to be retrained as an evidential network.
Regression coverage trade-offs.
The lower ID coverage observed for CLEAR on some regression
benchmarks arises because its uncertainty adjustment is driven by support in the calibrated latent
geometry. Samples that are only weakly supported by this geometry receive increased uncertainty,
which can include difficult or atypical ID examples as well as genuinely shifted inputs. In addition,
the operating threshold is selected to maximise ID-OOD separation rather than to enforce a fixed ID
retention rate, so improved rejection of OOD and adversarial inputs can be accompanied by reduced
ID coverage. Importantly, CLEAR preserves the underlying regression prediction, meaning that this
effect reflects a change in selective uncertainty rather than degraded predictive accuracy.

E.2
MODELS
To assess the performance and generalisability of CLEAR, we assess its performance against multiple
models and architectures across our experimental evaluation. Below, we provide a concise description
of each model together with the implementation details and hyperparameter settings adopted in our
experiments:
• ResNet-18 (He et al., 2016): is used for the MNIST classification and image-based regres-
sion experiments. We train it from scratch, setting the initial convolution to stride one and
removing the initial max-pooling layer for small images. The task-specific head is trained
for 10 epochs, followed by 40 epochs of backbone training. The resulting model is frozen
before CLEAR is applied.

rejecting OOD inputs, avoids ad hoc threshold tuning, and enables clear reporting of ID and OOD
coverage under deployment-like conditions.
Following calibration, the selected threshold is fixed and applied to the corresponding test sets. During
evaluation, any test input whose score under the chosen metric crosses this threshold is rejected. This
provides a consistent basis for comparing ID retention and OOD rejection across all models and
datasets.

E.4
COMPARATIVE APPROACHES
To evaluate the effectiveness of CLEAR, we compare it against a range of recent EDL and uncertainty
quantification approaches that are representative of the current state-of-the-art. Each approach is
briefly described below, alongside any implementation-specific details or hyperparameter choices
used in our experiments:
• Posterior Networks (Charpentier et al., 2020): provide a foundational approach to
uncertainty estimation in classification that predates and informs later evidential deep
learning methods. They avoid requiring OOD data during training by directly modelling
a closed-form posterior over categorical distributions. Specifically, the model predicts
Dirichlet concentration parameters α = βprior + β for each input x, where β denotes pseudo-
counts obtained from class-conditional density estimates in a learned latent space and βprior
is a fixed symmetric prior. The resulting Dirichlet distribution represents both aleatoric and
epistemic uncertainty in closed form.
• Evidential Deep Learning (EDL) (Sensoy et al., 2018): provides a deterministic alternative
to Bayesian neural networks for uncertainty estimation by modelling class probabilities
through a Dirichlet distribution whose parameters are derived from the model’s non-negative
outputs. The model is trained using an objective that combines squared prediction error,
predictive variance, and a KL divergence to a uniform prior. This evidential formulation
enables both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty to be quantified in closed form without
requiring sampling or OOD examples during training. EDL serves as the base approach for
the subsequent comparative methods. As the base method, its hyperparameters are shared
across the remaining comparative approaches to ensure a fair comparison and are described
in Appendix E.6.
• Fisher Information-Based Evidential Deep Learning (I-EDL) (Deng et al., 2023):
extends EDL by incorporating the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) to adaptively weight
the loss according to the informativeness of the predicted evidence. The central idea is
that classes associated with greater evidence contain less Fisher information and should
therefore be regularised less strongly. The method introduces a regularisation term based on
the log-determinant of the FIM to discourage overconfident predictions:

• Hyper-Opinion Evidential Deep Learning (H-EDL) (Qu et al., 2024): extends classical

inputs. The method introduces an opinion projection mechanism that maps hyper-opinions
to standard Dirichlet-based predictions, allowing training within the conventional EDL
framework. This projection also addresses the vanishing gradient problem that can limit
traditional EDL on fine-grained tasks. In our experiments, we adopt the two-stage training
procedure described in the original paper without introducing additional hyperparameters.

• Regularized Evidential Model (RED) (Pandey & Yu, 2023): addresses the inability of
conventional evidential models to learn effectively from samples mapped to zero-evidence
regions. RED introduces a vacuity-guided correct-evidence regularisation term that encour-
ages greater evidence for the ground-truth class, with its influence strongest when evidence is
low. This enables the model to learn from samples that would otherwise produce negligible
gradient updates while retaining the standard evidential learning objective.
• Smoothed Evidential Deep Learning (S-EDL) (Kopetzki et al., 2021): improves the
robustness of standard evidential models by applying median smoothing to their uncertainty
estimates. Given an input x, a collection of noisy samples {xs} ∼N(x, σ) is generated.
The final uncertainty estimate is obtained by taking the median across these samples,
improving robustness to adversarial perturbations by reducing the influence of outliers. In
our experiments, we follow the original setup and use a Gaussian noise scale of σ = 0.01
with 50 samples per input, based on guidance from the original paper and our manual tuning.
S-EDL is a purely post-hoc method.
• Conflict-aware Evidential Deep Learning (C-EDL) (Barker et al., 2026): is a lightweight
post-hoc extension of EDL that improves OOD and adversarial robustness without retraining
the base model. C-EDL generates multiple label-preserving transformed views of an input
and measures disagreement between their evidential outputs using intra-class variability
and inter-class conflict. The resulting conflict score is used to reduce evidential strength
when disagreement is high, increasing uncertainty while largely preserving predictions for
consistent ID inputs.
• Deep Evidential Regression (DER) (Amini et al., 2020): extends evidential learning to
continuous prediction by placing a Normal-Inverse-Gamma (NIG) distribution over the
mean and variance of a Gaussian likelihood. A single network predicts the NIG parameters
(γ, ν, α, β), where γ represents the predictive mean and the remaining parameters determine
the evidential strength and associated aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty. Training combines
the negative log-likelihood of the resulting Student-t predictive distribution with an error-
dependent evidence regulariser, which penalises high evidence when the prediction is
inaccurate. This enables both forms of uncertainty to be obtained analytically from a single
forward pass without requiring sampling or OOD examples during training. The setup is
described in Section 3. In our experiments, we use an evidential regularisation weight of
λreg = 10−3.
• Uncertainty Regularised Evidential Regression Network (UR-ERN) (Ye et al., 2024):
extends DER by addressing the high uncertainty area (HUA), in which the constrained
activations used to ensure valid evidential parameters can produce vanishing gradients
and prevent highly uncertain samples from contributing effectively to learning. UR-ERN
introduces an additional uncertainty regularisation term that maintains a learning signal
within this region, with its influence increasing with the discrepancy between the predicted
value and ground truth. This allows the model to move samples away from poorly learned
high-uncertainty regions while retaining the original evidential regression objective and NIG
uncertainty formulation. In our experiments, we use an uncertainty regularisation weight of
10−3.
• Evidential Meta Model (EMM) (Shen et al., 2023): is a post-hoc uncertainty approach
that combines representations from multiple frozen intermediate layers using a lightweight
evidential meta-model. We adapt EMM to object detection by applying ROIAlign to
predefined detector features for each predicted bounding box and parameterising a Normal-
Inverse-Gamma distribution over its four localisation coordinates. The meta-model is trained
for 20 epochs using Adam with an initial learning rate of 10−2, cosine annealing to 10−6,
an IoU matching threshold of 0.5, evidential regularisation weight λreg = 0.1, and gradient
clipping at 1.0.
E.5
ADVERSARIAL ATTACKS
This section presents the adversarial attack strategies used to assess the robustness of uncertainty-
aware models. Adversarial attacks introduce carefully constructed, often imperceptible perturbations
that can cause a model to misclassify while remaining highly confident. These attacks are particularly
challenging for uncertainty-aware methods because they can disrupt both aleatoric and epistemic
uncertainty signals, causing adversarial inputs to appear in-distribution or evade uncertainty-based
detection.
34


To provide a comprehensive robustness evaluation, we consider both gradient-based and gradient-free
attacks. Gradient-based attacks represent white-box settings, where access to model gradients is used
to construct adversarial perturbations. Conversely, gradient-free attacks apply random or structured
perturbations without relying on gradient information and more closely reflect black-box settings.
Where supported, attacks are implemented using Foolbox (Rauber et al., 2017) under the following
configurations:
• L2 Projected Gradient Descent (L2PGD) (Madry et al., 2018): is an iterative, white-box
adversarial attack that perturbs inputs within a bounded L2-norm ball to maximise the
model’s loss. At each iteration, the input is updated in the direction of the gradient of the
loss with respect to the input, followed by projection back onto the L2-ball of radius ϵ. This
results in smooth, high-precision perturbations that remain less perceptible to humans:

• Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) (Goodfellow et al., 2015): is a single-step white-box
adversarial attack that perturbs the input in the direction of the sign of the gradient of the
loss:
x′ = x + ϵ · sign(∇xJ(x, y))

• Salt & Pepper Noise: is a non-gradient-based black-box perturbation that randomly sets a
proportion of input pixels to their minimum or maximum value. This form of structured
noise simulates impulsive corruption and tests the model’s resilience to sparse, high-intensity
artefacts. It does not rely on model gradients and is agnostic to internal model parameters.
• AutoAttack L2 (Croce & Hein, 2020): is an ensemble of complementary adversarial
attacks comprising Auto-PGD with cross-entropy and difference-of-logits-ratio losses, the
Fast Adaptive Boundary attack, and the black-box Square Attack. Adversarial perturbations
are constrained by ∥x′ −x∥2 ≤ϵ. The attacks are applied sequentially to inputs that
remain correctly classified, combining gradient-based and query-based strategies to evaluate
robustness without model-specific hyperparameter tuning.
E.6
TRAINING DETAILS
To ensure that differences between uncertainty methods are not driven by method-specific optimi-
sation, we use a common training configuration wherever possible. All models are trained with a
batch size of 64 using the Adam optimiser with weight decay 10−4. For MNIST, CIFAR-10, and
Oxford Flowers, training follows the two-stage procedure described above: the task-specific head
is first trained with the feature extractor frozen, followed by joint fine-tuning of the backbone and
head. The head is initially trained with a learning rate of 10−3; during fine-tuning, the backbone and
head use learning rates of 10−5 and 10−4, respectively. For ImageNet, the ImageNet-1K pretrained
ViT-B/16 backbone remains frozen and only the task-specific head is trained, using a learning rate
of 10−4. The tabular MLP uses a learning rate of 10−3 throughout. Learning rates are reduced by a
factor of 0.5 when validation loss does not improve for five epochs, with a minimum learning rate of
10−8. A dropout rate of 0.25 is used in the shared predictive architecture. These optimisation settings
are kept fixed across comparative approaches for a given dataset, with method-specific parameters
varying only where required by the corresponding uncertainty formulation.
